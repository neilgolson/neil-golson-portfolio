Tesla · SolarCity · Retail Demand & Conversion · 2014–2018

Building SolarCity's retail demand engine, then running it on the numbers

I joined SolarCity in April 2014, about 16 months after its IPO. Revenue had been about $160M the year before. By 2016, when Tesla bought the company, it was $730M. My job was to find homeowners ready to buy solar at a lower cost per install than digital. Home Depot turned out to be the answer, and about half of sales came through that one channel. After the acquisition I ran the other end of the funnel at Tesla: turning a Model 3 purchase into an energy purchase.

The insight

Purchase decisions happen in moments

A homeowner in the plumbing aisle pricing a water heater is already thinking about energy bills. A Model 3 buyer who just put down a deposit is already running ten-year numbers. In both moments, solar is the next logical question.

"The best pitch answers the question the buyer is already asking."

The job was to be standing there when that question came up, and to know what it cost us to be there. Find the moment, put a rep in it, measure the cost of every install that came out of it.

Act one · The demand engine

Go where homeowners are already spending on their homes

Door-to-door and digital were expensive ways to find buyers. Home Depot had something neither one had: homeowners standing in the store, already spending money on their houses.

Big box worked because of context. A rep can ask, "What are you working on today?" and get a real answer. Then they can walk the customer to what they need and pitch solar in a way that ties to that project. I'd managed a Home Depot store earlier in my career, so I knew how much that first question opens up.

We built the channel to 800 Home Depot stores, with Best Buy and other partners alongside, and staffed it with a 1,500-person field team.

Act one · The funnel and the math

About 20 rep-hours for every installed system

The goal was one opportunity per rep-hour. Reps averaged about half that. About 20% of opportunities closed, and about half of those closes made it to install. The rest fell out to operational issues and cold feet.

Reps earned about $40K base and about $70K at target. That put rep-level CAC at about $700 to $750 per installed system. Add managers and Home Depot's share and the fully loaded number was about $1,000. Digital ran about $1,500.

At about $100M a year in field payroll, small changes in rep productivity moved real money. So we kept changing things.

Act one · Measure, change, verify

Fewer hours, more installs

In the bottom 10% of stores, we cut staffing to Saturday and Sunday only. Opportunity rates rose to quota, one an hour. Close rates climbed from single digits to the 20% national average. We stopped paying reps to stand in empty aisles on weekdays.

That was one turn of a loop we ran constantly. We measured each store, changed something, and checked that the change happened before we trusted the numbers.

01 · Measure
Read every store
Opportunities per rep-hour, close rate and install rate, store by store.
02 · Change
Pull a lever
Hours and days, reps per store, the store list, the table, the script.
03 · Verify
Secret shop it
Confirm reps actually ran the change before reading the result.

↻ Back to measure

The levers were plain. We moved the hours and days we staffed. We changed how many reps worked each store. We added stores that performed and dropped the ones that didn't. We got Home Depot's own store associates to send customers our way. We redesigned the table and the kiosk more than once.

We rewrote the script over and over. A new script only counts if reps say it, so we secret shopped stores to confirm they did. Without that step, a bad number could mean a bad script or a script nobody used, and we'd have no way to tell which.

Act one · Channels I cut

Two channels that didn't pay back

The same math that scaled Home Depot shut other channels down. Two didn't survive the numbers.

Cut · Mall kiosks
Too general
Mall shoppers weren't in the middle of a home project. Reps had no project context to work with and no natural way to start the conversation, which is the whole advantage of a big box store.
Cut · AT&T / DirecTV
The partner's reps didn't engage
Their reps did their own job and wanted to get out. A few figured out how to sell solar alongside it. Not enough to scale.
Act one · The partner side

1,500 people inside someone else's stores

Every rep worked for two audiences at once. Home Depot's shoppers came in for their own projects, and our reps had to help with those first. Home Depot's leadership and store managers had to keep seeing us as good for their shoppers and their stores, or we'd lose the floor space.

In 2015 Home Depot named SolarCity its Environmental Partner of the Year.

The channel scaled under SolarCity and reached 800 stores under Tesla in 2018.

Act two · The conversion layer

Turning a car purchase into an energy purchase

The demand engine found buyers. At Tesla, the job was converting a different buyer: someone already in a store for a car. Vehicle and energy had been running as parallel tracks, with different spaces, different advisors and different conversations. A customer could leave with a Model 3 having never heard about Powerwall, even if they would have bought it.

We merged the tracks for the Model 3 launch. The Solar + Powerwall energy wall went onto the sales floor. Advisors could pull up a household energy profile, linked in the CRM, in the middle of a vehicle conversation. Test drives were paired with energy consults, and come-to-you drives included a home walkthrough. Online booking fed one CRM that treated the car and the house as one household decision.

30%+ Model 3 buyers who added energy
50–100% Sales productivity lift vs. best non-Tesla retail locations

Solar needs a separate install, separate financing and a separate decision. More than 30% of Model 3 buyers added it anyway, because the conversation was already happening.

Act two, cont. · Product launch

Solar Roof: commercializing a product that didn't exist yet

In October 2016, Elon Musk unveiled the Tesla Solar Roof on a residential street in Los Angeles: glass tiles that work as the roof and generate power.

I managed early VIP customer relationships and the commercialization process, turning a public demo into an orderable, installable, warrantied product. Those customers put down deposits on something that didn't ship yet. My job was keeping them bought in through a long development timeline while feeding their demand signals and feedback back into the product and install pipeline.

What came next

The through-line from Tesla to what followed

The Tesla and SolarCity chapters taught me something that transferred directly to what came next. The parallels are hard to miss: a category-defining company, a product most people hadn't bought before, a brand that needed to earn credibility fast in a market that didn't have a brand leader yet. FlashParking was all of those things, just in B2B / B2G parking technology instead of consumer energy.

At Tesla I learned that the retail experience has to do the trust work advertising can't. At SolarCity I learned how to build a field organization that could scale that trust across hundreds of locations without losing conversion quality. At FlashParking, both of those capabilities (physical presence, field-level credibility, brand as the thing that gets you on the shortlist before the RFP) applied directly. A parking technology company with no brand, no category, and less than 1% market share went to 33% and a $1.1B valuation. The toolkit was the same. The category was different.
