Realtor.com · 17-Brand Housing Coalition · Sept–Nov 2026

Seventeen competitors, one housing message, six weeks to Election Day

The U.S. is 4 million homes short. In June 2026 Congress passed the ROAD to Housing Act, and the next move fell to city councils and county boards, many of them on the November ballot. Let America Build 2.0 turned that into a race: the communities that permit, zone and build first win. Realtor.com led 17 real estate brands, Zillow included, to put housing supply in front of voters in the six weeks before the midterms.

The problem

A federal housing law that can't permit a single home

The ROAD to Housing Act passed in June. It can fund housing and reward the places that build it. It can't approve a permit, rezone a lot or shorten a review timeline. Local governments do that, and in 2026 a lot of the people who decide were up for election.

So the job was narrow and hard: get housing supply onto the local ballot in six weeks, in a midterm year, without picking a party. A housing campaign that reads as partisan loses half its audience and most of its partners on day one.

"Houses don't just appear. We have to build them."

The answer was a race. Communities compete for jobs, schools and federal dollars every year. Framing housing the same way gave every candidate, from any party, a way to win it, and gave voters five specific things to ask for: permit more homes, cut red tape, fill empty spaces, secure the federal grant money, and build modern.

The coalition

Brands that compete every other day of the year, on one letter

We launched with 15 brands and wrapped with 17. Realtor.com and Zillow signed the same CEO letter in the Wall Street Journal, as "colleagues and competitors." So did NAR, NAHB, Habitat for Humanity, ICON, eXp, HomeServices of America, Veterans United, NextHome, T3 Sixty, RISMedia, Inman, AREAA, NAHREP, the LGBTQ+ Real Estate Alliance and Land Use Labs.

That list is the strategy. A housing message from one portal is advertising. The same message signed by its competitors, its builders and its trade press reads as an industry position, and editors treat it like news.

Every partner got a co-brandable toolkit so the campaign ran through their channels as well as ours. The two brands that joined mid-flight are the number I watch most: partners signing on after launch means the work was worth attaching your name to.

The creative

A glass house on a red pickup, driven through Boston

The housing shortage is invisible until you can't find a place to live. We made it something you could see from the sidewalk: a glass-walled house frame on the back of a red pickup, touring Boston on Sept. 23 and 24 with the line "Houses don't just appear, we have to build them."

The tour became the film. A two-minute documentary opens on seventeen brands taking one message to the streets, then cuts to :30 and vertical versions for YouTube and Meta from Oct. 1.

Around it: full pages in the Wall Street Journal and New York Post, homepage takeovers across the Post and Fox Weather, News Corp display and video, Metro Report Cards on local housing supply, and lawn signs.

My job was keeping it pointed at the vote. I pushed the platform to "the race to let America build" and cut copy that celebrated before the work was done. A line like "We'll win this race together" sounds good in a review and asks nothing of the reader.

What it moved

An industry position the press covered as news

The campaign earned 400+ media mentions and more than $5M in earned media value, and Realtor.com's CEO called it out in the company's Q3 shareholder update as the work it's doing on the market's biggest structural problem.

What generalizes is the coalition math. A category leader can buy reach. It can't buy the credibility of its competitors standing next to it, and on a policy issue that credibility is the whole campaign.
